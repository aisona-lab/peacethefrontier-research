## The question is which risk, under which conditions

AI safety is not a single score. A system can produce discriminatory decisions, disclose confidential information, assist fraud, fail in a critical workflow, or create a more speculative danger through autonomous behaviour. These mechanisms need different evidence and different remedies. Combining them into one probability can hide the people affected and the institutional responsibilities involved. This review separates demonstrated harms, laboratory findings, forecasts and policy choices. It is an editorial review of selected sources checked on 13 November 2026, not a systematic review or a legal opinion.

The International AI Safety Report 2026 is the main research synthesis used here. Published in February and commissioned by more than 30 countries, it covers capabilities, risks and risk management. Its assessment distinguishes malicious use, malfunctions and systemic effects; it also describes substantial gaps in the ability to evaluate advanced systems. A synthesis can show where evidence converges without supplying a universal certification that a particular deployment is safe.<Nota f="iasr-2026" />

## Demonstration does not establish prevalence

The report discusses harms involving synthetic content, manipulation, cyber misuse and other applications. Evidence that an attack or failure can occur establishes a possibility under tested conditions. It does not, by itself, tell us how often it occurs, how much additional harm AI caused relative to existing tools, or whether a proposed defence works in ordinary deployment. Those questions require denominators, counterfactuals and observation over time. Incident counts also depend on reporting: more reports can reflect better detection as well as more harm.

For this atlas, the practical reading rule is to ask what outcome was measured and whose circumstances were represented. A simulated attack, an account from a provider and an independently documented incident should remain visibly different. We do not reproduce operational instructions for harmful applications. The useful research questions concern access controls, detection, accountability and the effectiveness of safeguards, rather than instructions for causing the underlying harm.

## Capability evaluations are necessary and incomplete

The international report describes improvements in capabilities relevant to autonomous operation, alongside unreliable performance and limitations in evaluation. A test measures a particular model, its tools, prompting, available time and scoring rules. Changing any of these can change the result. Evaluating the base model alone can miss risks introduced by a connected agent; evaluating a powerful agent with extensive assistance can overstate what a typical user obtains.

The report also discusses models recognising evaluation settings. This is a reason to investigate whether test behaviour transfers to deployment, not proof that every favourable result is deliberately deceptive. Independent access, test diversity, reproducible protocols and reporting failed attempts can improve scrutiny. These are editorial recommendations consistent with the report's concerns; the evidence does not establish that any single checklist eliminates serious failures. Evaluation should inform a decision about a specified use, with explicit residual uncertainty.

## Loss of control is a distinct, uncertain claim

At its February 2026 assessment, the report concluded that existing systems lacked the capabilities to pose loss-of-control risks while improving in relevant areas. That is a dated assessment, not a guarantee about every subsequent release. It does not imply that existing systems are harmless: misuse and ordinary failures do not require an AI system to escape control. Conversely, an improvement on an autonomy benchmark does not demonstrate an impending global catastrophe.

Long-term probabilities are judgments under uncertainty. They depend on definitions, time horizons and assumptions about future development and governance. Our separate [catastrophe article](/en/articles/risk-of-catastrophe/) explains the forecasting questions used in the model. We do not convert those answers into measured annual frequencies. A responsible interpretation permits precaution about severe outcomes while distinguishing the strength of evidence for the mechanism, its likelihood and the effectiveness of an intervention.

## What the European framework currently says

The European Commission's AI Act overview, read during this review, distinguishes prohibited practices, high-risk uses, transparency obligations and general-purpose models. It reports that prohibitions and AI literacy obligations applied from 2 February 2025 and general-purpose model obligations from 2 August 2025. It describes transparency obligations applying in August 2026. These are categories with different scopes, not a single date on which every AI application acquires the same obligations.<Nota f="ue-ley-ia" />

The same page reports revised high-risk deadlines of 2 December 2027 for specified Annex III uses and 2 August 2028 for systems embedded in regulated Annex I products, following the AI Omnibus. This is a material change from summaries of the original timetable. We attribute these dates to the Commission's guidance: the linked Official Journal amendment did not return readable legal text in this review. Verification against the operative legislation and any applicable transition provisions remains pending. Readers making compliance decisions must consult that legislation rather than treating this draft as a determination of their duties.

## Rules, standards and enforcement are different evidence

The Commission describes an AI Office and national authorities with supervisory responsibilities, together with supporting institutions and guidance. It describes the general-purpose AI Code of Practice as a voluntary compliance tool. A code, a harmonised standard, a binding regulation and an enforcement decision are different instruments. Their existence does not establish the observed effectiveness of enforcement or prove that an individual system satisfies its duties.

An editorial follow-up should therefore examine implementation: access to documentation, capacity to evaluate models, treatment of serious incidents, remedies for affected people and published decisions. These are questions for future evidence collection, not claims that the corresponding mechanisms already work well. We also distinguish an announced programme from an operational service. Policy announcements are useful primary evidence of what an institution intends, but outcomes require a different kind of source.

## The Council of Europe adds a rights framework

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law opened for signature on 5 September 2024. Its official explanation covers principles such as equality, privacy, transparency, accountability and safeguards for people affected by AI systems. It describes risk and impact assessments, procedural rights and a follow-up mechanism involving the parties.<Nota f="coe-convenio-ia" />

This treaty is distinct from the European Union's AI Act. Signature, ratification and entry into force are separate legal events. The overview lists participants, but this review has not independently checked every country's status in the treaty office register; we make no blanket claim that all signatories are bound by provisions currently in force. The official explanation also identifies exclusions and national-security qualifications. Describing the convention as universal regulation of all AI would conceal these limits and the choices available in domestic implementation.

## Power affects who can scrutinise a system

The international report discusses systemic risks as well as individual model failures. Our editorial interpretation is that safety oversight also depends on who can obtain information, commission evaluations, challenge decisions and change suppliers. Compute, data, distribution and institutional access are distinct potential sources of power. They should not be collapsed into an unsupported numerical concentration index or inferred from one firm's benchmark lead.

There are tradeoffs. Centralised services may offer coordinated updates and controlled access, while also creating dependency on a small number of decision makers. More widely accessible systems may support independent research and adaptation, while making some controls harder to enforce. Neither arrangement is inherently safe in every context. A useful comparison specifies the threat, the affected community, access to remedies and the evidence for actual mitigation. This review does not estimate global market shares or claim a causal relationship between concentration and a measured catastrophe rate.

## Oversight needs an evidence trail

For a consequential deployment, we recommend a record of the intended use, tested population, known failure modes, review responsibilities and criteria for stopping or changing the system. Independent evaluation has value when evaluators can inspect relevant evidence and publish material limitations. Independence should be assessed through funding, access and publication arrangements; a label alone cannot settle it. These are governance recommendations, not findings that every institution currently follows such practices.

People affected by decisions need usable routes to challenge them. The convention's discussion of information and procedural safeguards provides a normative reference for that objective. However, publishing a model card or adding a human approval step is not itself evidence of meaningful oversight. Follow-up research should ask whether the person can understand the limitation, has authority and time to intervene, and whether the intervention improves outcomes. Formal responsibility without practical capacity can leave the underlying failure unchanged.

## How this relates to the futures model

The atlas explores an assumed relationship between cooperation, concentration of power and cumulative catastrophe risk. Its risk coefficients are calibrated or assumed; they are not estimated causal effects from the AI Act, the convention or the safety report. Changing the peace slider does not simulate a particular legal reform. The four futures are conditional narratives for exploring priorities, not competing forecasts assigned objective probabilities.

We preserve the equations and numerical inputs in this refresh. Neither a new law nor a more capable model supplies a scientifically matched replacement for an annual hazard coefficient. The sensitivity bands show how outputs move within the selected parameter ranges. They exclude many structural uncertainties and cannot justify a precise claim about the probability that a named policy prevents catastrophe. A stronger causal model would require a separate redesign, clearly specified mechanisms and evidence beyond this editorial review.

## What would change this assessment

The most useful updates would include independent evaluations across deployment settings, transparent incident data with meaningful denominators, evidence about safeguard durability and published enforcement outcomes. Legal updates require checking the operative text, commencement dates and jurisdiction. A new report should be compared with the earlier edition rather than silently replacing it: changed definitions can resemble changed trends.

This draft leaves explicit unknowns. We cannot assign a validated probability to loss of control, establish the global effectiveness of current governance, or infer the distribution of future power from capability scores. The priority is a traceable account of what was observed, what an institution says, what is forecast and what we recommend. Monthly editorial checks will revisit rapidly changing policy and evaluation evidence; automation can flag links, but it cannot decide whether a new result supports a stronger conclusion.
