## What is measured

To train an AI model, a computer performs an enormous number of mathematical operations. That number, training compute, measures resources used in training. It does not measure what the model can do, but models with more compute tend to be more capable.

A peer-reviewed study gathered the compute of models since 1950. Before 2010 it doubled every 20 months, in line with Moore’s law; with the arrival of deep learning it began doubling roughly every 6 months.<Nota f="sevilla-2022" />

## The historical pace through May 2024

Epoch AI, the group that maintains that database, estimated in 2024 that the compute of notable models grew about 4.1 times a year between 2010 and May 2024, and that of frontier models about 5.3 times. Since 2018 the frontier has grown closer to 4 times a year, and language models about 5 times since mid-2020. Their summary: 4 to 5 times a year. This is a historical estimate, not a new measurement for November 2026.<Nota f="epoch-computo" />

Growing 4 to 5 times a year for a decade multiplies compute by between one and ten million.

## Where it comes from

In Epoch’s 2023 hardware analysis, the performance per dollar of AI GPUs doubles every 2.1 years, less than 1.4 times a year.<Nota f="epoch-hardware" /> Computational performance doubled every 2.3 years in that sample, distinct from price-performance. Neither rate measures improvements in algorithms or general capability.

## Where the chips are made

A 2024 Council on Foreign Relations analysis reported that more than 90% of the most advanced chips were manufactured in Taiwan; the figure is historical and depends on the category of advanced chips.<Nota f="cfr-taiwan-2024" /> A January 2026 CFR analysis described US rules allowing some advanced chip exports to China under restrictions. This is a dated policy account, not verification of the rules in force today.<Nota f="cfr-chips-2026" /> Compute depends not only on technology but also on geopolitics.

## What it means for the futures

In the [futures model](/en/futures/), the maximum frontier uses a historical training-compute reference of 4.5 times a year, and the minimum, about 1.35 times a year, is what chip performance alone improves: it doubles every 2.3 years. That is why the [today point](/en/futures/#hoy) sits at the right edge of the matrix. Mapping compute growth to capability is an assumption. The “today” marker uses this historical reference; values outside the matrix are clipped for display. The model omits explicit algorithmic-efficiency changes and growth above its chosen endpoint.

## What we do not know

No exponential trend lasts forever. Energy, chip manufacturing and money set limits, and we do not know when they will bite. Nor do we know how much more capable a model becomes for each multiplication of its compute: compute measures effort, not results.
