## The finding worth keeping

The International Labour Organization’s 2025 occupational-exposure index estimates that one in four workers worldwide is in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI. It places 3.3% of global employment in its highest exposure category. These are estimates of exposure to tasks a technology could affect, not counts of people dismissed or predictions of unemployment.<Nota f="oit-genai-2025" />

That distinction should organise the debate. A job contains tasks, relationships, responsibilities and institutional constraints. A tool that changes part of the task bundle can alter a job without eliminating it. Equally, demonstrating that some human tasks remain does not prove that employment, bargaining power or income will remain unchanged.

The purpose of this review is to connect three kinds of evidence: estimates of technical exposure, measured effects in workplaces, and changes in the conditions under which those effects are measured. They answer different questions. The atlas should show their connections without pretending that one number can substitute for all three.

## How exposure is constructed

The ILO working paper combines task information, worker input, expert assessment and model predictions. Its description reports a representative sample drawn from 29,753 tasks in the Polish occupational classification, a survey of 1,640 workers, and further expert assessment before mapping scores to international occupational categories. It explicitly updates an earlier 2023 index.<Nota f="oit-genai-2025" />

This is a structured estimate, not a census of machines performing every task. The translation from task descriptions to occupational scores introduces judgment. International comparisons also depend on how well the classification represents work in different labour markets. That makes definitions and methodology essential to interpreting an attractive headline percentage.

The ILO describes job transformation as the more likely overall impact because most occupations retain tasks requiring human input. Readers should understand this as the study’s assessment, rather than a guarantee for every worker or firm. The index identifies where change may be important; it does not measure how rapidly organisations will adopt tools or how they will distribute the gains.

## Exposure is distributed unevenly

The index finds relatively high exposure in clerical occupations and increased exposure in some highly digitised professional and technical roles. Its highest category covers 4.7% of female employment and 2.4% of male employment globally. It also reports a larger overall exposure share in high-income countries than low-income countries: 34% versus 11%.<Nota f="oit-genai-2025" />

These differences concern the occupational distribution and the study’s classification. They should not be translated into equivalent differences in future unemployment. Adoption, infrastructure, labour protections, demand and organisational choices can change the path from exposure to outcomes. The same technology can arrive in workplaces with very different constraints and alternatives.

The distributional question is therefore more specific than whether AI “creates or destroys jobs.” Which tasks change first? Who can access training? Who controls how tools are used? Who receives the benefits of increased output, and who bears the burden of review or intensified work? Answering those questions requires evidence about institutions as well as software.

## A productivity gain in customer support

The NBER working-paper version of *Generative AI at Work*, revised in November 2023, studies the staggered introduction of an assistant among 5,179 customer-support agents. Its abstract reports a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour, with a larger improvement among novice and lower-skilled workers and little effect among the most experienced workers.<Nota f="nber-ia-trabajo-2023" />

The version matters. The NBER page links a journal publication from 2025, but the statistics stated here are those of the accessible working-paper version. A reader should not assume that every sample count and estimate is identical across versions. Preserving a versioned citation avoids quietly combining results from different papers.

The outcome also matters: issues resolved per hour in a particular customer-support setting. It is evidence of a productivity gain in that application. It does not directly measure national GDP, the effect on all service jobs, or the number of workers a company will employ after demand responds. Productivity and employment are separate outcomes even when one influences the other.

## Why another study found slower work

METR’s July 2025 randomised study examined sixteen experienced open-source developers completing 246 tasks in familiar repositories. Access to the early-2025 AI tools increased task completion time by 19% on average. The researchers describe it as a snapshot in one relevant setting, not a general verdict on all programming assistance.<Nota f="metr-desarrolladores-2025" />

The customer-support and developer findings are not interchangeable. The people, task structure, available context, tools and outcome measures differ. One evaluates a specific support workflow; the other involves skilled developers working within systems they know. Comparing them helps identify heterogeneity, but averaging their percentages would not yield a meaningful estimate of “AI productivity.”

A useful interpretation asks which part of the workflow improves and which part becomes more demanding. Drafting or coding can be faster while verification, integration or correction takes longer. The appropriate unit is the completed task at an acceptable quality level, including the work needed to discover mistakes.

## What the 2026 follow-up changes

METR’s February 2026 update reports difficulty interpreting its later developer experiment. Some participants were reluctant to work without AI, compensation changed, and simultaneous use of several agents complicated measurement. The authors say the resulting data give an unreliable signal of the current productivity effect.<Nota f="metr-productividad-2026" />

The raw estimates suggested possible speedups for returning and newly recruited participants, but the intervals included no improvement. The authors considered increased usefulness plausible while emphasising that selection could make the observed sample unrepresentative. This is evidence about the limits of the experiment as well as about the tools.

Consequently, the older 19% slowdown should not be presented as the current effect of every AI coding tool. Nor should the follow-up be presented as a definitive reversal. A good update preserves both findings and explains why the newer estimate does not settle the question. Sometimes research improves our understanding by exposing a measurement problem.

## From a task to an organisation

Even a well-estimated task-level effect does not automatically become an organisation-level gain. A company must integrate the tool with information systems, procedures and quality standards. Some work becomes a bottleneck elsewhere. Time saved by one person may require review by another. These are mechanisms to examine, not a numerical estimate that this review has measured.

Deployment decisions should therefore specify what counts as useful output and which costs belong in the comparison. Counting generated text, accepted suggestions or tool usage may be informative, but none is necessarily the final business outcome. A lower cost per attempt can coexist with a higher cost per accepted result if failure and review are expensive.

For an organisation evaluating adoption, the practical recommendation is to measure complete workflows before and after introduction, with a credible comparison group where possible. Include quality, correction, employee experience and the distribution of effects. Averages alone can conceal workers who benefit substantially and workers for whom the tool creates additional work.

## Productivity does not determine wages by itself

The cited studies do not establish how economy-wide wages or employment will evolve. Those outcomes depend on demand, competition, bargaining institutions and how organisations use productivity gains. Higher output per hour can support several responses: lower prices, greater output, fewer hours, new tasks or changes in staffing. Which response occurs is an empirical and institutional question.

This is why a forecast based only on technical capability is incomplete. A system’s ability to perform a task does not determine who owns it, who can afford access, who is liable for mistakes or who negotiates its introduction. Those conditions influence whether benefits become broadly shared or concentrated.

The atlas treats redistribution, institutional coordination and power concentration as scenario choices. They are not effects measured by the ILO exposure index or the workplace studies. Linking these sources to a scenario provides context; it does not turn the scenario’s coefficients into observations.

## Training, entry-level work and autonomy

A finding that novices benefit in one setting raises questions about learning, rather than resolving them. Does assistance help people acquire skills they can later use independently? Does it change which tasks new workers are allowed to practise? Does it reduce routine burdens or remove useful learning opportunities? The support study reports evidence relevant to learning, but it cannot settle these questions for every occupation.<Nota f="nber-ia-trabajo-2023" />

The same caution applies to worker autonomy. A tool can support judgment or become a means of closer monitoring, depending on its design and use. That distinction requires evidence about the workplace arrangement. It should not be inferred solely from whether the underlying model is described as powerful or efficient.

A constructive research agenda would follow people over time, compare different implementation practices and measure outcomes beyond immediate throughput. It would also include workers whose jobs are changing, rather than relying only on the organisations purchasing the technology.

## Implications for the atlas

The futures model’s automation variable is a simplified scenario quantity. It does not equal the ILO’s share of exposed workers. Updating one with the other would mix task potential, employment counts and a dynamic modelling assumption. The review therefore updates the explanation and citations while preserving the numerical model inputs.

The most useful ongoing indicators are comparable exposure updates, adoption by occupation and firm size, measured workflow outcomes, job transitions and distributional effects. None should be treated as a substitute for the others. A coherent picture needs both technical and social evidence.
