## What changed in the evidence

The International Energy Agency’s April 2026 update estimates that data centres consumed 485 TWh of electricity in 2025 and projects around 950 TWh in 2030, close to 3% of global electricity demand. It reports 17% growth in total data-centre electricity consumption during 2025, with faster growth in AI-focused facilities. These are different categories: the total includes services that have nothing to do with generative AI.<Nota f="aie-ia-2026" />

This updates the agency’s 2025 report, which estimated 415 TWh for 2024 and projected 945 TWh in its 2030 base case. The small change between the two 2030 projections does not mean little has happened. The observation year, project pipeline and information available to the forecasters have changed. Keeping both editions lets a reader distinguish an updated assessment from a supposedly precise prediction.<Nota f="aie-energia-ia" />

The central finding is that demand is growing while the composition of that demand is changing. Neither a headline about efficient chips nor a headline about a giant campus settles the environmental question. The relevant quantities are useful computing services, electricity use, the timing and location of supply, and emissions over the infrastructure’s life.

## Four quantities that must remain separate

Training compute counts operations used to develop a model. Inference is the work performed when that model answers requests or executes tasks. Data-centre electricity includes servers, cooling and other infrastructure across many applications. Primary energy is a different accounting concept again. A trend in one of these quantities cannot simply be pasted into another.

Epoch AI’s May 2024 analysis described roughly four-to-fivefold annual growth in training compute over particular historical samples. The article’s measurement window ends in May 2024. It is evidence about that historical trend, not a measurement of total AI electricity in November 2026, and not proof that model capability rises by the same factor.<Nota f="epoch-computo" />

A model can therefore use a historical compute trend as a scenario input, provided it explains the mapping. A research website should also state what would invalidate the extrapolation: financing constraints, hardware supply, changes in training methods, different model sizes or a shift in spending toward inference. These are possible mechanisms, not measured contributions that can be assigned percentages without further evidence.

## Efficiency has several denominators

The 2023 Epoch hardware study reported different trends for different metrics. For the hardware sample studied, computational performance doubled approximately every 2.3 years, price-performance every 2.1 years, and energy efficiency every 3 years. These numbers cannot be used interchangeably. A cheaper operation, a faster chip and a less energy-intensive operation answer different questions.<Nota f="epoch-hardware" />

The IEA’s 2026 assessment also describes large reductions in energy per AI task alongside expanding demand and more energy-intensive applications. That does not establish a universal energy cost for an “AI query.” A short text answer, a long reasoning sequence, video generation and an agent making repeated tool calls can consume very different amounts of computation.<Nota f="aie-ia-2026" />

An honest efficiency comparison therefore holds the task, output quality, hardware boundary and measurement conditions as constant as possible. Otherwise a change in the task mix can look like an efficiency improvement or deterioration. A useful service becoming cheaper can also encourage more use. This is a reason to measure total demand alongside efficiency, not a reason to assume that every efficiency improvement is cancelled by rebound.

## Read projections as conditional statements

The older IEA report explicitly examined alternative futures. Its 2035 base case was about 1,200 TWh, while a higher-efficiency case and faster-expansion case produced different outcomes. The point of those alternatives is to expose dependencies on adoption, software and hardware improvements, and infrastructure constraints. They are not confidence intervals generated by repeatedly observing the future.<Nota f="aie-energia-ia" />

The 2026 update keeps the near-term central outlook broadly similar but emphasises supply-chain and grid bottlenecks. It also discusses upside possibilities after 2030 if constraints ease and more energy-intensive uses spread. An atlas should retain that tension: current constraints can slow deployment without establishing a permanent ceiling on long-term demand.<Nota f="aie-ia-2026" />

When comparing editions, ask whether both use the same definition of a data centre, the same geographical coverage, the same treatment of AI-focused facilities and the same projection horizon. A different number is not necessarily a revision of the same measurement. This review uses the agency’s global data-centre totals and labels the observation years explicitly.

## The grid is local even when the headline is global

A modest share of world electricity can still be a large addition to a particular grid. The 2025 IEA report discusses geographical concentration and connection delays; it estimates that grid constraints could delay a portion of planned projects. This helps explain why global totals alone cannot establish the local effect on reliability, prices or public infrastructure.<Nota f="aie-energia-ia" />

The 2026 update adds detail about power density, storage, connection constraints and possible on-site generation. A project announcement is not electricity delivered to a server. Permits, equipment, construction, interconnection and operations stand between an announced capacity and an observed load. Counting every announced project as operational would turn an investment pipeline into a false measurement.<Nota f="aie-ia-2026" />

For a local decision, the useful questions concern who pays for network upgrades, how the facility behaves during scarcity, whether demand can shift in time, and which customers bear the consequences of delays. Those are questions for project-specific evidence and policy choices; the global reports do not answer them for every location.

## Electricity demand is not a carbon footprint

Climate impact depends on both electricity consumption and the emissions associated with supplying it. The IEA’s 2026 assessment projects data-centre-related emissions reaching around 350 million tonnes in 2035, approximately 2% of electricity-sector emissions in that projection. The denominator matters: this is not 2% of all human greenhouse-gas emissions.<Nota f="aie-ia-2026" />

Renewable procurement is relevant evidence, but a contract does not by itself describe the supply serving every hour of demand. Similarly, an efficient server does not establish the emissions of its electricity. The reader needs the accounting boundary, period, geography and assumptions used in the comparison. Embodied impacts of equipment and construction should not be silently mixed with operational electricity estimates.

Possible benefits also need a boundary. Using AI to optimise an energy system may reduce resource use in a particular application, but an estimate of technical potential is not proof of savings already realised across an economy. Both the computing cost and the measured improvement belong in a comparison. Claimed benefits should survive a credible comparison with what would have happened without the AI system.

## What the atlas model does—and does not—say

The atlas retains a 2026 starting estimate of 550 TWh inherited from interpolation of the older IEA figures. It is not an observed 2026 AI-only total. Updating the article with the 2026 report does not magically turn that historical modelling choice into a measurement. The parameter table now distinguishes estimated inputs from observed, derived, calibrated and assumed values.

The model also imposes a ceiling equal to a chosen share of global electricity and links demand to an abstract capability variable. Those are scenario assumptions. A ceiling can dominate the long-run output, making two otherwise different futures converge. Such convergence should be read as a property of the equations and assumptions, not independent evidence that the world will converge.

There is an additional accounting limitation: the model adds a quantity of electricity, converted to exajoules, to a primary-energy baseline using the substitution method. Reconciling the boundary and possible double counting requires a separate model review. This refresh documents that limitation and preserves the equations. A transparent unresolved limitation is more informative than a recalibration that hides the mismatch.

## What remains uncertain

Public reporting does not provide a complete, consistent breakdown of all training, inference and non-AI services. Changes in utilisation and task composition can matter as much as peak hardware specifications. Company disclosures may use different boundaries, making apparently simple comparisons difficult. The IEA calls for better disclosure and continued monitoring rather than treating one report as a final answer.<Nota f="aie-ia-2026" />

Long-run projections compound uncertainties in demand, supply, technology and policy. This does not make them useless. It changes their purpose: they can identify pressures worth preparing for and assumptions worth testing. A scenario should help a reader recognise the evidence that would change the conclusion.

For this atlas, the practical watchlist is the next comparable IEA assessment, operational rather than announced capacity, independently documented task-level energy measurements, and changes in power supply and grid constraints. These items should be reviewed together. A new benchmark score alone cannot update a national electricity forecast.

This is an editorial review of identified primary reports, not a systematic review of all energy research. The IEA editions and Epoch analyses were consulted on 13 November 2026. Historical findings retain their original measurement windows. The article remains a draft for editorial review, and the simulation remains an illustrative set of futures.
